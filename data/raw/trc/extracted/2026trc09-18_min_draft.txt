4. PRESENTATION BY KARL FRIEDEN AND DYLAN WAITS (COUNCIL ON
STATE TAXATION) TITLED “A NATIONAL PERSPECTIVE ON HAWAII'S
STATE AND LOCAL TAXES: A LOOK AT EXCISE TAXES,
ADMINISTRABILITY, AND CORPORATE TAXES”:
Overview
This was the second external presentation in the Commission’s planned series. COST provided a
comprehensive comparative review of Hawaii’s tax structure relative to national norms, with
particular focus on:
• Composition of state and local taxation
• Business tax burden data
• Hawaii’s General Excise Tax (GET)
• Administrative best practices
• Corporate income tax structure

Major Themes & Discussion Points
National vs. Hawaii Tax Composition
• Hawaii relies heavily on consumption taxes, particularly the GET, compared with
national averages.
• Hawaii’s property tax share is much lower relative to other states, largely due to the
structure of the state economy and relatively limited industrial base.
• While Hawaii’s income tax system is progressive, reliance on consumption taxes places
Hawaii near the national middle in overall regressivity.
Business Tax Burden
• Hawaii’s business share of total state/local taxes is near the national average.
• COST emphasized the importance of looking beyond corporate income tax—property
tax, excise tax, and business input taxation are more influential in business tax burden.
General Excise Tax (GET) Structure
Key observations:
• Hawaii’s GET has an exceptionally broad base, similar only to South Dakota and New
Mexico.
• The broad GET base allows Hawaii to capture revenue from emerging digital sectors
without new laws but also creates significant pyramiding, taxing goods and services
multiple times along supply chains.
• Pyramiding leads to effective tax rates higher than the statutory 4–4.5%, depending on
industry structure.
• Most states exempt many business inputs to mitigate pyramiding; Hawaii exempts very
few, making pyramiding a central concern for business taxpayers.
Digital Taxation Trends
• Many states are slowly expanding sales tax bases to digital products and software. Hawaii
is already far ahead due to the GET’s broad reach.
• COST cautioned against separate digital advertising taxes; Hawaii already taxes such
activities within the GET.
Administrative Practices
COST identified areas for potential improvement that are revenue-neutral:
• Extend corporate filing deadlines to align with federal extended deadlines.
• Adopt longer appeal periods (moving from 30 days to the recommended 90 days).
• Improve federal audit adjustment conformity.
• Ensure even-handedness in interest rates on assessments vs. refunds.

Corporate Income Tax
• Hawaii’s CIT structure (rate, water’s-edge reporting, exclusion of GILTI/“NECTY”)
places it roughly in the national mainstream.
• Hawaii’s use of three-factor apportionment stands out, as most states have shifted to
single-sales-factor apportionment.
Commission Questions
Commissioners asked about:
• Effective tax rates after pyramiding
• Equity and competitiveness implications
• Relationship between Hawaii’s economic composition and tax structure
• Treatment of flow-through entities
COST responded with detailed analysis of pyramiding studies, national comparisons, and
Hawaii’s unusual structural profile.

5. PRESENTATION BY DR. JOHN KNOX TITLED “TOURISM, TRANSIENT
ACCOMMODATIONS TAX (TAT), AND COMPETITIVENESS”:
Overview
Dr. Knox presented an extensive data-driven review of Hawaii’s tourism trends and the historical
interplay between visitor taxes (TAT and GET) and visitor behavior.

Major Themes & Discussion Points
Tourism Market Composition
• Hawaii is now overwhelmingly dependent on the U.S. West and U.S. East markets (over
80% of visitor volume and spending).
• Japan and other international markets have fallen dramatically.
Visitor Tax Burden Over Time
• Combined GET, county surcharges, green fees, and TAT now total 18.71%, among the
highest lodging tax burdens nationwide.
• Historical increases in TAT and reallocation of revenues have steadily shifted funds
toward the general fund and away from tourism marketing or management.
Historical Correlation Analysis
Dr. Knox reviewed correlations between tax rates and tourism outcomes:
• Visitor arrivals: Positive long-term correlation; tax increases did not correspond with
declines.
• Visitor census (average visitors present): Same positive correlation, though recent
decreases reflect shorter stays.
• Length of stay: Weak long-term correlation; declines since 2011 not clearly tied to tax
changes.
• Real visitor spending: Near-zero correlation with tax rate changes.
• Daily spending: Historically negative correlation (higher taxes, lower daily spending), but
reversed upward after the pandemic, indicating higher per-day expenditures.
Dr. Knox emphasized that tax changes tend not to move major tourism metrics on their own.
Shift in Visitor Spending Patterns
• Growing share of spending is directed to lodging, possibly influenced by rising lodging
taxes and fees.
• Shopping has declined as a spending category.
Visitor Attitudes
Visitor satisfaction surveys continue to show:
• “Too expensive” remains the top reason for not planning a return trip.
• Post-pandemic concern about costs has increased.
Use of TAT Revenues
• Based on national comparisons, Hawaii allocates very little of lodging tax revenue to
tourism-related uses (roughly 2.5% vs. a national average of 41%).
• Hawaii allocates far more to the general fund (about 72%).

Dr. Knox noted long-standing industry concerns that tourism-related revenues are not reinvested
into tourism competitiveness or management.
Policy Considerations
Knox encouraged the Commission to consider:
• Ensuring stable revenue streams for tourism marketing and management
• Tracking all tourism-related revenues (including fees and minor taxes)
• Possible guidelines for TAT allocation by category
• Acknowledging political resistance to major structural changes in TAT use or rate
Commission Discussion
Commissioners asked about:
• Variations in tax impact across hotel market segments
• Reliability of visitor data (especially front/back of arrival forms)
• Possible explanations for rising average daily spend
Dr. Knox clarified current data availability, noted that average party size has slightly increased,
and discussed potential methodological concerns with electronic arrival forms.
