Testimony of the Hawaiʻi Tax Fairness COalition
Support for Revenue Options That Enhance Hawaiʻi’s Competitiveness
Tax Review Commission
Wednesday, October 28, 2026, at 2:00 PM
Dear Chair White, Vice Chair Chock, and Members of the Commission:
Thank you for the opportunity to testify on behalf of the Hawaiʻi Tax Fairness Coalition in support
of revenue options that would make Hawaiʻi's tax system more competitive—through lowering
the cost of living for working families.
Competitiveness and Affordability
The Commission has considered how Hawaiʻi can improve the "competitiveness" of its tax
system without losing revenue.3 We assert that a truly competitive tax system supports the
working families who form the backbone of our economy. Achieving that goal requires raising
tax revenue from the households best able to pay, and investing that revenue in programs and
services that lift up Hawaiʻi’s low- to middle-income population. Examples include tax credits that
deliver cash assistance, along with workforce development.
The evidence from Massachusetts’ millionaire’s tax proves that taxing high earners does not
drive them out of state. Massachusetts began charging a 4 percent surtax on income above $1
million in 2023, and it collected about $3 billion from the surtax in fiscal 2025. According to IRS
data, the net out-migration of Massachusetts residents earning $200,000 or more actually fell in
the surtax's first year.4
Conveyance Tax Reform and Competitiveness
The marginal-rate reform described here follows SB3028, the conveyance tax bill the
Legislature considered in 2026. Its final draft would have moved residential sales to marginal
rates, adjusted the tax on multifamily properties to reflect value per unit, indexed the rates to

===== page 2 =====
inflation, and directed part of the revenue to the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands. The bill
went to conference committee but did not pass.5
Reforming the conveyance tax serves to make Hawaiʻi more competitive and affordable in three
ways:
First, raising the conveyance tax will not cause wealthy homeowners to leave Hawaiʻi. As
UHERO’s draft report for this commission states, “Other things equal, taxes do the least
damage when the base cannot easily shrink, move or hide.” This affirms the importance of
taxing tangible assets that are tied to land.6 The conveyance tax is charged once, when a
property is sold, at a small fraction of the total value. Much of the tax falls on homes that are
sold to non-resident buyers—not local residents.
Second, reform would fix two flaws in today's conveyance tax. One is the cliff effect: a sale just
under $1 million would currently owe about $2,000, while a sale at exactly $1 million would owe
$3,000. A marginal tax system would resolve that issue, while lowering taxes for resident
homeowners in the process.6
Third, reform would help retain working families, who are increasingly forced to leave due to the
high cost of living. The Commission's scope includes workforce retention and affordability,3 and
UHERO's draft report notes that a tax change could do little for residents if housing stays
scarce.6 Representative Luke Evslin noted that the proposal would have lowered taxes for 94
percent of local buyers. Millions of dollars would have been raised to fund the construction of
affordable housing, including funding for the Department of Hawaiian Homelands.7
We also would like to advocate for a controlling-interest provision in the conveyance tax, which
would treat the sale of a controlling stake in a company that owns Hawaiʻi real estate in the
same manner as a sale of the property itself. This would close a loophole that allows some
wealthy individuals to avoid paying the conveyance tax altogether.
Other Revenue Options
In addition, we urge the Commission to consider other revenue options that raise taxes on
wealthier households, such as taxing capital gains at the same rate as ordinary income. Hawaiʻi
taxes long-term capital gains at a maximum rate of 7.25 percent, while the top income tax rate

===== page 3 =====
will reach 13 percent in tax year 2027. Capital gains are overwhelmingly earned by the
wealthiest taxpayers in the state, and raising the capital gains rate would have little effect on
working families. In 2022, over 70 percent of the capital gains in the state went to taxpayers
earning $400,000 or more.⁶
In all, the revenue could pay to extend the 2023 expansions of Hawaiʻi's refundable tax credits,
which end after tax year 2027. Since these credits pay working families even when they owe no
income tax, they help families put food on the table and continue living in Hawaiʻi.⁶
Mahalo for the opportunity to testify.
Devin Thomas
