Dr. Moore’s presentation covered the philosophy, structure, and analytical approach of the
UHERO report:
a. Overall Framing and Themes
• The report will emphasize responsiveness of tax bases (behavioral, timing, mobility,
enforcement sensitivity).
• Spillover effects—particularly for business taxation—will appear throughout the
analysis.
• A hierarchy of tax responsiveness (land → labor → capital gains → business capital →
wealth) was outlined to contextualize expected behavioral reactions.
b. Topics to Be Included (Breadth of Issues)
Dr. Moore noted the report will address a wide range of tax areas raised during Commission
meetings, even those added after UHERO’s engagement. These include:
• Business taxation (corporate income tax; pass-throughs; apportionment methods)
• Single sales factor analysis (conceptual, with limited Hawaiʻi-specific estimation due to
data constraints)
• Estate tax and wealth tax (limited treatment, largely conceptual and focused on
enforcement difficulty and behavioral responses)
• Conveyance tax and differential property taxation
• Tax pyramiding and TAT impacts (with ongoing modeling work)
• Tax credits (renewable energy, film production, EITC, food/excise credit, capital goods,
etc.)
• Visitors’ vs. residents’ tax burdens
Dr. Moore clarified that some topics would receive only brief attention in the draft, with more
detailed treatment in the final report.
c. Hard Numbers vs. Conceptual Analysis
Some reforms will include quantitative modeling, including revenue-neutral scenarios. Others,
such as tax pyramiding, TAT, and certain property tax questions, will need additional time for
modeling and will appear more fully developed in the final report.
Dr. Moore presented an example: exempting groceries from the GET, which UHERO estimates
would reduce state revenues by approximately $267 million, including about $53 million
attributable to tourist spending. Distributional effects differ by income, with higher-income
households capturing the largest dollar savings but a smaller share of their income.

d. Tax Credit Evaluation Framework
Dr. Moore proposed a set of guiding questions for evaluating tax credits (e.g., purpose,
beneficiaries, counterfactual behavior, costs/benefits, alternative mechanisms). Commissioners
noted the framework’s usefulness but raised concerns about its practicality for policymaking.
e. Competitiveness
Commissioners emphasized that “tax competitiveness” was intended as the overarching theme
of the consultancy scope. Dr. Moore acknowledged this and agreed to ensure competitiveness is
woven more explicitly into the report's narrative and analytical framing.
f. Administrative Feasibility of Credits
Commissioner Enos inquired about administrative burdens (e.g., AGI [adjusted gross income]
certification for solar credits). Dr. Moore agreed this is important but noted limited available
research; he will explore potential sources for comparison.
g. Additional Commissioner Feedback
Chair White discouraged spending excessive time on wealth tax analysis, as the Commission had
largely dropped the topic. Dr. Moore acknowledged and noted such sections would remain brief.
Commissioner Fukunaga supported including wealth tax material because of national relevance.
Commissioner Katakura reiterated the need for clear analysis on competitiveness.
Commissioner Enos asked whether UHERO would compare Hawaiʻi's policies to other states;
Dr. Moore confirmed comparative context will be included where meaningful.
Chair White raised interest in including treatment of the research tax credit, which Dr. Moore
agreed could be incorporated into the final report.
Dr. Moore concluded by inviting continued feedback during October as UHERO finalizes its
report.
h. Additional Comments from Former Commissioner Dr. John Knox
Dr. Knox cautioned that Commission recommendations sometimes encounter political obstacles
and asked whether the UHERO report could comment on political feasibility. Dr. Moore
responded that, while he can flag general considerations, he lacks sufficient Hawaiʻi-specific
political history to provide strong guidance in this area.

6. SCHEDULING OF FUTURE MEETINGS
A lengthy scheduling discussion followed, due to multiple travel schedules, tax deadlines, and
other conflicts.
Ultimately the Commission agreed on the following:
• October 8, 2026, 2:00–4:00 PM (partial attendance with quorum expected)
• October 20, 2026, 2:00–4:00 PM (partial attendance with quorum expected)
• October 28, 2026, 2:00–4:00 PM (full-Commission meeting; primary follow-up
