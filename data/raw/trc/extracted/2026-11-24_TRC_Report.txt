REPORT OF THE 2025-2027 TAX REVIEW COMMISSION
INTRODUCTION
The Role and Authorization of the Tax Review Commission
The Tax Review Commission’s role is clear. Evaluate the State’s tax structure and recommend
revenue and tax policy to the Legislature. The Tax Review Commission (Commission) is then
dissolved and reconstituted after five years’ time.1 The Hawaii Revised Statutes, Chapter 232E
provides further guidance and directs the Tax Review Commission to systematically review Hawai‘i’s
tax structure using standards such as equity and efficiency.2
The Commission received further guidance from the Legislature. The Senate adopted Resolution 193
and the Legislature adopted House Concurrent Resolution 182.4
Senate Resolution 19 requested the Commission to “identify possible means by which the Hawaii
Long-Term Care Financing Program can be implemented.” While the Commission was deliberating,
Act 2495 was signed into law July 14, 2026 and directs the Executive Office on Aging to develop a
framework and scope of work for a proposed study to examine public and private financing options
for long-term care services. The Act also appropriates funds to the Executive Office on Aging for this
purpose. The Commission considers its role in developing recommendations for long-term care
financing to be superseded by Act 249. The Commission has chosen to leave further study of this
topic to the Executive Office on Aging.

topics, including considering the provisions of the Internal Revenue Code and other federal policies
and activities; ensuring non-residents and visitors contribute appropriately to our tax system;

Themes
The Commission’s vision for its overall package is competitiveness of Hawai‘i’s greater economy.
Within this vision, and regarding the principles of sound tax policy put forth below, the Commission
has been guided by the principle of tax adequacy. The Commission has tried to design its package to
avoid creating revenue losses that will add to the already difficult decisions the Legislature must
make. The Commission acknowledges the annual duty of the Legislature to provide for the State’s
residents while balancing the State’s budget.
More granularly, the Commission’s recommendations are guided by the principles of tax equity and
efficiency. Guidance from these two principles can lead to discovery of some tax policy changes that
may not seem like much, but in aggregate can add up over time.
Accompanying the Commission’s package on competitiveness is a set of standalone
recommendations addressing specific issues within Hawai‘i’s tax structure. Following that, the
Commission describes some less granular, but more grand, ideas that may be worthwhile but
ultimately require research and study beyond the Commission’s scope.

The tax system must raise enough revenue, whatever enough is. This is particularly important for
states as they typically have balanced budget requirements and cannot utilize deficit financing.12
Related to the principle of tax adequacy is how robust the tax system is. The concern is whether the
tax system break down over time due to changes to the economy. This is always possible, but in

as a share of the economy by transition to a service-based economy.13 This may not have been
foreseeable in the 1930s when sales taxes were being enacted, but its continuance is foreseeable
today. Hawai‘i’s General Excise Tax does not have this weakness.14
Additionally, adequacy and robustness are important at this time due to the reduction in federal
funding provided to the states, described earlier.15
Neutrality
The principle of neutrality provides that taxes should have as little effect on market decisions as
possible. This principle is mostly visibly violated by the many incentives and disincentives contained
in tax codes like credits, deductions, exemptions, and special rates.16
Horizontal and Vertical Equity
The concept of horizontal equity holds that similarly situated taxpayers should be treated equally.
The concept of vertical equity holds that tax burden should increase as ability to pay increases.17 In
other words, vertical equity measures how the tax burden is distributed along the income
spectrum.18
Horizontal equity is more economically measurable and definable than vertical equity. Economic
theory provides less guidance when it comes to vertical equity.19 Vertical equity is often left to
politics.20
Regressivity and progressivity of the tax system are related topics and are covered in more detail
below.

While we are guided by the above principles, we recognize that sound tax policy does not exist in a
vacuum. The principles of sound tax policy are a guiding light, but that light often must be refracted
by reality. There are reasons to violate the principles of sound tax policy. The simplest is to provide a
benefit to society other than the raising of revenue. For example, so-called ‘sin taxes’ in their most

===== page 6 =====
recognizable forms provide the benefit of discouraging the use of liquor or tobacco or any of an
assortment of other activities.
Sin taxes obviously violate horizontal equity. Sin taxes by definition apply to a subset of businesses
and individuals engaging in a subset of activities. Creating tax distinctions between businesses that
are equally situated but for society’s decision to disfavor one or the other industry obviously violates
horizontal equity. Given the widespread usage of sin taxes, this violation is clear worth it.
Tax exporting is another potential reason to violate the principles of sound tax policy. Tax exporting
refers to the ability of a jurisdiction to shift the burden of a tax from residents to nonresidents.21 Doing
so is nearly universally politically popular. Places with unique tourist attractions or natural resources
have increased opportunities to do so.22 The Commission’s recommendations, specifically those
regarding tourism or general excise tax, may violate the principle of horizontal equity or at least leave
existing horizontal inequities in place. However, exporting tax to nonresidents may be worth it.
Regressivity and Hawai‘i’s General Excise Tax
Regressivity and progressivity are perennial tax policy topics.23 The concepts are related to vertical
equity. A progressive tax system is one in which higher income taxpayers pay a larger percentage of
their income in taxes than do lower income taxpayers. A regressive tax system is one in which lower
income taxpayers pay a larger percentage of their income in taxes than do lower income taxpayers.
On a tax-type-by-tax-type regressivity and progressivity are much easier to measure. However, the
progressivity of the entire tax system is much more complex.24
The importance of Hawai‘i’s General Excise Tax cannot be overstated. It is the state’s most important
tax type.25 The reason for this is the breadth of the tax, but this is sometimes its greatest threat. A
side effect of a broad consumption tax like the GET is regressivity. The GET is a very regressive tax
type when viewed in isolation.26
But the breadth of the tax is its greatest strength. The run-of-the-mill sales taxes, typical in mainland
states, have steadily decreased in importance over the last few decades, finally being surpassed in
nationwide state-level importance by the individual income tax.27 Hawai‘i’s General Excise Tax,
however, did not see this fall, due to the breadth of its base. One reason for the declining importance

provides a better opportunities to address the regressivity of the GET. The income tax reform of 2024
used the individual income tax in this way.
DISCUSSION OF THE 2024 TAX REFORM – ACT 46, SLH 2024
Regressivity is an important issue and there are multiple ways to address it.
One is to raise the tax liability of the higher-income taxpayers. The other is to reduce the tax liability
of lower-income taxpayers. The former raises revenue; the latter puts money directly into the
pockets of the lower-income taxpayers. Act 46, SLH 2024 addressed these two levers.

Develop and Utilize a Framework for Analyzing Credits and Exemptions
Objective:
Recommendation:
Develop a framework or frameworks to analyze tax expenditures, including existing and proposed
tax credits and exemptions.
Narrative:
The efficacy of existing credits and exemptions is an evergreen topic for Tax Review Commissions.
Targeted tax incentives have been called the scourge of state tax policy.42 Targeted tax incentives are
available to only a small number of taxpayers despite their typical language suggesting they are
generally available.43 This is often accomplished through use of investment-size thresholds, jobs-
creation minimums, or the use of other criteria that appears general but is designed to benefit
specific taxpayers.44 On top of violating various principles of sound tax policy, many of which were
highlighted earlier in this report, the literature suggests that businesses do not respond to state tax
incentives anyway.45
It is important to note what has already been done. In 2016, a pair of Acts were passed requiring the
State Auditor to look at all credits and exemptions. These are referred to as Act 24546 and Act 261,47
or, the 2016 Acts.
• Act 245 (2016) for Income Tax and Financial Institution Tax
• Act 261 (2016) for GET, Use, Public Service Company, and Insurance Premium Tax
The 2016 Acts require the State Auditor to do seven things:
