4. TRC DISCUSSION OF THE UNIVERSITY OF HAWAI‘I ECONOMIC RESEARCH
ORGANIZATION (UHERO) DRAFT REPORT AND DEVELOPMENT OF
FEEDBACK TO UHERO
Chair White opened the main agenda item: discussion of the 172-page UHERO draft report
delivered the day prior. Members acknowledged the limited time to fully review the report but
agreed it was important to begin substantive discussion.
4.1 Initial Observations
Members briefly exchanged comments about the report’s length, complexity, and technical
density. Although no one had completed a full read, members emphasized the importance of
identifying themes, questions, and potential recommendations early.
4.2 Revenue-Neutral Tax Pairings (UHERO Report p.161)
Chair White noted UHERO’s list of preliminary “pairs of changes” intended to be revenue
neutral. These included possible exemptions (e.g., groceries) offset by rate increases. He
expressed a strong personal preference against exempting groceries from the GET, describing it
as a “red line” from a policy standpoint, while acknowledging he is one of seven votes.
Commissioners discussed whether such examples should remain in the report’s framing, with
several indicating interest in offering UHERO guidance on which policy options should receive
emphasis or further analysis.
4.3 GET Structure, Tourism Burden, and Visitor-Focused Taxation
Commissioner Fukunaga raised the question of whether the General Excise Tax (GET) could be
structured to capture more revenue from visitors, citing UHERO’s findings on the substantial
share of GET paid by tourists. Ideas discussed included:
• Evaluating mechanisms to adjust GET burdens without harming tourism.
• Examining historical issues such as tourism-related income-splitting provisions.
• Considering special districts (e.g., Waikīkī) with differentiated GET rates to reflect
visitor-oriented activity.
• Acknowledging historical constitutional concerns regarding differential treatment of
residents vs. non-residents (e.g., Commerce Clause and Privileges and Immunities
challenges).

Members agreed that the feasibility of such mechanisms is complex and should be modeled
cautiously.
4.4 GET Pyramiding and Economic Distortions
Commissioners engaged in an extended discussion on pyramiding in the GET, its distributional
and economic consequences, and possible reforms. Themes included:
• Whether reducing or restructuring wholesale rates could produce a more neutral, less
distortionary tax system.
• The administrative complexity of determining wholesale qualification for services.
• Potential impacts on different industries, including whether tourism-related purchases
would benefit or be burdened by reforms.
• The role of UHERO’s simulation model (under construction) in quantifying different
reform scenarios.
The Commission expressed interest in UHERO modeling several “what-if” scenarios related to
pyramiding relief and corresponding rate adjustments.
4.5 Tourism Impacts, TAT, and Elasticity of Visitor Demand
Chair White invited commentary from subject-matter contributor Dr. John Knox regarding the
Tourism and Transient Accommodations Tax (TAT) sections of the UHERO draft.
Key points raised:
• The UHERO draft has focused more on revenue mechanics than on tourism
competitiveness considerations.
• The concept of compensating residents for increased TAT burdens may be problematic
because only a subset of residents pay TAT as consumers.
• Estimating the elasticity of visitor demand in response to TAT increases is complex due
to:
– Multiyear booking patterns;
– Multiple simultaneous price components (airfare, room rates, fees);
– Small proportionate contribution of TAT to total trip cost.
• UHERO intends to update elasticity estimates in the final report; commissioners asked
that UHERO expand competitiveness benchmarking and segment analysis (luxury vs.
budget market impacts).
Commissioners discussed the general difficulty of isolating the effects of TAT increases but
noted that updated modeling would be valuable before the Commission considers any
recommendation involving TAT rates.

• Only about $150M constitutes what might be considered “economic development

6. Estate Tax Discussion and Report from Meeting with the Governor
Commissioner Fukunaga reported on a meeting held that morning with the Governor, joined by
Chair White. The Governor expressed interest in exploring ways Hawaiʻi might:
• Attract high-net-worth individuals, particularly if states like California adopt wealth
taxes;
• Encourage investment or charitable contributions that produce local economic benefits;
• Potentially adjust or “pre-pay” estate tax mechanisms tied to beneficial economic
activity.
• Commissioners noted:
Such reforms would require significant further study and careful attention to
o
cultural sensitivities surrounding wealthy in-migrants’ land ownership and
community impacts.
The concept is likely too complex to develop fully within this Commission’s
o
timeline but may warrant inclusion as a “topic for future study” in the final report.
Any recommendation must ensure public benefit and avoid merely providing
o
preferential tax treatment detached from measurable economic gains.
7. Structure of the Commission’s Final Report
Chair White shared preliminary thoughts on organizing the Commission’s Final Report,
including:
• An introductory fiscal overview (federal funding volatility, post-COVID shifts).
• A primary section on tax competitiveness, synthesizing UHERO analysis and
Commission recommendations.
• A secondary section covering Commission-initiated topics, such as retirement income,
single-sales-factor apportionment, conveyance tax issues, estate tax considerations, and
others discussed in prior meetings.
• A third section for items requiring further study (e.g., estate tax incentive structures,
Waikīkī district taxation, potential combined-reporting reforms).
He noted the absence of a dedicated TRC researcher/writer this cycle and acknowledged that he
may need to prepare initial drafting, with commissioners contributing topical memos as was done
in the previous cycle.
