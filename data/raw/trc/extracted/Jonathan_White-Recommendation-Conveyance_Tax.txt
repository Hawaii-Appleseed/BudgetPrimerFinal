Conveyance Tax Controlling Interest:
Objective:
To increase the equity and efficiency of Hawai‘i’s Conveyance Tax by adding a provision that will
trigger the tax upon the transfer of entities holding interests in real property.
Proposed Recommendation:
Add a provision that would trigger the conveyance tax if a buyer purchases a controlling share of a
company or trust that owns real estate in the state.
On Effectiveness:
One issue considered by the Commission was the possibility that a conveyance tax applied to
controlling interests would be only marginally more difficult to avoid than the current conveyance
tax. This is a valid concern. Every law creates a way around itself. However, it is difficult to believe
that the other seventeen states with controlling interest provisions are just being walked all over. The
Commission’s recommendation below is built from the practices of several states’ taxes and should
ensure broad but not perfect application.
Nonetheless, the effectiveness of states’ controlling interest provisions is difficult to judge. A 2018
proposal from New York proposed to expand the state’s controlling interest provision. The expansion
would have applied the conveyance tax to all transfers of an interest in an entity that held New York
real estate if real estate comprised fifty percent or more of the entity’s total value after the transfer.
In other words, the requirement of a transfer of fifty percent or more of the voting shares of an entity
would be gone for these entities. The change was estimated to produce an additional $5 million per
year. The point of this is not to recommend it for Hawai‘i. The point is only to highlight that the
controlling interest provision must be taxing at least some transfers of controlling interests if there is
a proposal to expand it in this way that is also estimated to raise revenue.1
There will be tax planning; some of it successful.2 The exact amount is impossible to predict.
Regardless, the Commission believes improvement of horizontal equity justifies imposing a tax on
the transfer of a controlling interest in real property.
Narrative and Detail:
Hawai‘i’s conveyance tax applies to the transfer of real property interests.3 However, the tax does not
apply to the transfer of an interest in an entity that itself owns a real property interest. This is a
violation of horizontal equity. The Commission believes the conveyance tax should apply to all
transfers of real property whether the interest is held by an individual or a legal entity.
Seventeen jurisdictions agree and apply a controlling interest provision within their conveyance
taxes, while fourteen jurisdictions do not impose a real estate conveyance tax at all.4 Thus, slightly

Jurisdictions have done this using two distinct approaches. One approach is to trigger the
conveyance tax on any transfer of a controlling interest in an entity holding real estate. The
jurisdictions using this approach include Connecticut, New Jersey, New York, Minnesota, Maine, and
Washington. The various bills considered in Hawai‘i all proposed this type of imposition. For this

interest laws and a recent Hawai‘i proposal follows.
New Jersey:
New Jersey’s controlling interest transfer provisions are considered among the best and most
straightforward of any state.6 New Jersey’s law contains less than 1,200 words in total.
The law contains the standard and near universal definition of a “controlling interest.” Under the
definition, a controlling interest is, for a corporation, more than fifty percent of the total combined
voting power of all classes of stock, and for a partnership, association, trust, or other organization,
more than fifty percent of the beneficial ownership of classified real property of that partnership,
association, trust, or other organization.7 All of the laws surveyed below contain similar definitions
of a “controlling interest.”
New Jersey’s law contains language to include a “direct or indirect” transfer.8 The inclusion of both
direct and indirect transfers ensures taxation of the transfer of an upper tier entity that owns no
property itself but instead owns a lower tier entity that owns the interest in real property.9
New Jersey’s tax base depends on the classification of the property. The tax base is the sales price if
the entity holds only “classified real property” but is the property’s equalized assessed value (for
property tax) if the entity owned other property in addition to “classified real property.10 “Classified
real property” is a defined term and, in general, includes “commercial properties.”11
The use of a property tax assessment value avoids administrative issues that can arise when both
real property and other property types are involved. In stock sale or membership interest
transactions, there may be disagreement and disparate incentives over how much of a sales price to
assign to the real property assets and the other assets.12 New Jersey’s use of an option between sales

New York:
New York imposes tax on each conveyance of real property or interest therein when the consideration
exceeds $500.21 New York’s law has been described as one of the best and most straightforward in
the nation.22
New York’s law defines a “controlling interest” in the common way. In the case of a corporation, a
controlling interest is either fifty percent or more of the total combined voting power of all classes of
stock of such corporation, or fifty percent or more of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in such
voting stock of such corporation. In the case of a partnership, association, trust, or other entity, a
controlling interest is fifty percent or more of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in such
partnership, association, trust or other entity.23
New York’s administrative rules provide that a transfer of a controlling interest includes a transfer by
parties acting in concert. The rules provide that persons are considered to be acting in concert when

Washington:
Washington imposes a real estate excise tax that applies to the transfer of a controlling interest of
fifty percent or more.31 The definition of controlling interest is similar to that of other states. For “profit
corporations,” the measure is fifty percent or more of the total combined voting power of all classes
of stock entitled to vote, or, fifty percent of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in the voting stock
of the corporation. For any other corporation, or a partnership, association, trust or other entity, it is
fifty percent or more of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in such entity.32
The Washington law provides for different methods of calculating the tax base, similar to New
Jersey’s law. The tax base is the “true and fair value” of the real property owned by the entity and
located in Washington.33 However, if total consideration for the sale, or the value of the property,
cannot be reasonably determined, the “market value assessment” from county property tax records

Washington’s law provides that the transfer of a controlling interest includes the transfer or
acquisition, within any 36-month period, of a controlling interest in any entity with an interest in real
property in Washington for a consideration.36
Washington’s law also provides for the aggregation of transfers made in concert. The language
determining whether parties have acted in concert is similar to the language used by New York. When
there is common ownership, the question is whether there is a relationship such that one person
influences or controls the actions of another through that common ownership. When there is no
common control or ownership, the question is whether the way in which the transfer is negotiated
and consummated supports that the parties are acting as a single entity.37
Washington’s law provides the common exemption for a transfer that is a mere change of identify or
form of ownership.38 Washington’s law also provides an exemption for a transfer that does not involve
the recognition of gain or loss for entity formation, liquidation or dissolution, and reorganization.39
However, this exemption is backed up by a limiting provision that denies the exemption where a
transfer precedes the exempt transfer, or follows the exempt transfer, by thirty-six months if the other
transfers result in the transfer of a controlling interest for valuable consideration, and in which one
or more persons previously holding a controlling interest receive cash or property.40
Washington’s law requires reporting of transfers of controlling interests41 and specifically provides
for the application of existing penalties for failure to report a transfer of a controlling interest.42
Connecticut:
Connecticut also imposes tax on the transfer of a controlling interest.43 The definition of “controlling
interest” is based on the common definition. In the case of a corporation, a controlling interest is
more than fifty per cent of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock. In the case of a
partnership, association, trust, or other entity, a controlling interest is more than fifty per cent of the
capital, profits, or beneficial interest in such entity.44 The tax base is the “true and actual value of the
property.45
Connecticut’s law provides that transactions within six months of one another are presumed to be
part of a series of transactions.46 The law provides that a taxable transfer includes a sale made by a

A Previous Hawai‘i Proposal and Specific Commission Recommendations:
In 2026, the Legislature considered H.B. 1918.49 H.B. 1918 proposed to impose tax on transfers of
controlling interests generally, as did all of the Hawai‘i bills the Commission surveyed. The
Commission considers this bill a great starting point and suggests a few amendments below.
H.B. 1918 defined “controlling interest” for corporations, and separately, for partnerships,
associations, trusts, and other entities. For corporations, a controlling interest is fifty percent or more
of the total combined voting power of all classes of the corporation’s stock, or, fifty percent or more
of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in the voting stock of the corporation. For partnerships,
associations, trusts, and other entities, a controlling interest is fifty percent or more of the capital,
profits, or beneficial interest in the partnership, association, trust, or other entity. This language is
standard language and shared across many jurisdictions’ controlling interest provisions.50 The
Commission recommends this language be maintained.
H.B. 1918 would have applied the conveyance tax to transfers that result in (1) the transfer of a
controlling interest in an entity with an interest in real property located in the Hawai‘i for valuable
consideration; and (2) the transfer of control or ownership of the real property located in Hawai‘i.
The proposed imposition would have applied to a transfer or series of transfers “by any person or
entity acting alone or in concert, within any twelve-month period.” The bill provided that persons are
acting in concert when there is a relationship between them such that one person influences or
controls the actions of another through common ownership. The bill further provides that persons
are only considered to be acting in concert when the way in which the transfer is negotiated and
consummated supports that they are acting in concert.51
These words are very similar to language used in other states’ laws, for example New York, but the
meaning is different here. In other states, these two clauses are two independent ways in which
persons can be found to be acting in concert. In H.B. 1918, the conjunctive “and” is used between
these two clauses. This means the second clause would restrict the application of the first. This
means that to be acting in concert the parties would have to have a relationship such that one
influences the other through common ownership and the way in which the transfer is negotiated and
consummated would have to support that they are acting in concert.

The Commission recommends using this language in the way other states use it. This will make
Hawai‘i’s law more consistent with other states.
H.B. 1918 contained one provision that is different from other states. The bill provided an explicit
statement that under some circumstances a series of transactions would be considered separate
acquisitions. The Commission recommends maintaining this language.
H.B. 1918 defined the tax base as the fair market value of the real property owned by the entity at the
time the controlling interest was transferred or acquired. Fair market value was defined as the value
of real property as stated on the most recent real property assessment. The Commission
recommends using the tax base determination used by New Jersey and Washington. This method
provides that the tax base is either the price paid for the property, or, if that cannot be reasonably be
determined, the most recent assessed value used for property tax.
The bill excluded from taxation any transfer consisting of only a change in identity or form of
ownership of an entity where there is no change in the beneficial ownership. This includes transfers
to an entity wholly owned, directly or indirectly, by the same common ownership as the transferor.
This is a very common exemption.52 The Commission recommends this exemption be maintained.
The Commission recommends considering two exemptions provided in New Jersey’s law. The first is
for transfers that are incidental to a corporate merger or acquisition if the equalized assessed value
of the real property transferred is less than 20 percent of the total value of all assets exchanged in the
merger or acquisition.53 The second is for transfers that are intercompany transfers within a
combined group that constitutes a unitary business.54
H.B. 1918 does not contain an exemption for transfers that are solely a change in controlling interest
if the people that the transfer is between have held their interests for three years or more immediately
before the transfer. Such an exemption was suggested in testimony.55 The Commission recommends
considering an exemption for a conveyance for certain transfers involving projects for which a low-
income housing tax credit has been issues. An exemptions for this was considered in prior bills.56
The bill provides for taxation of the transfer of a controlling interest at the lowest rate of conveyance
tax in certain circumstances. These items are removed from the current list of exemptions and are
not unrelated to the controlling interest provisions. However, the language of these applications at
the lowest rate mirror some of the exemptions provided in other states’ laws.57 The Commission
recommends considering converting these to outright exemptions.

Hawai‘i proposal recommended requiring a certificate of conveyance with transfer of a controlling
interest.58 The Commission recommends requiring this, or something similar, as a way to ensure a
paper trail is created to track taxable transfers. Washington’s law addresses this by requiring all
transfers of controlling interests to be reported to the state,59 and specifically providing for the
application of existing penalties for failure to report.60
The Commission recommends relying on New Jersey’s forms and administrative rules as well as New
York’s administrative rules and guidance. New Jersey’s forms are considered the leading examples61
and New York’s guidance is extensive.
The Commission recommends imposing the tax on transfers taking place on or after January 1 of the
year following the year after the change is made. For example, if the Legislature amends the law in
2027, the recommended imposition date would be January 1, 2029. This will give the Department,
Bureau of Conveyances, landowners, and planners ample opportunity to adjust and prepare.
The Commission recommends considering transition language to safe-harbor agreements entered
into before the effective date of the bill. This would be the effective date of the bill, not the imposition
date of the tax. Presumably, this would be the date the bill was signed by the Governor, the date it
became law without the Governor’s signature, or, some other date chosen and assigned by the
Legislature. The earliest this date would be is the date the law became effective and the latest date
would be the tax imposition date.
