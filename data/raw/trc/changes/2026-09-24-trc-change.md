PRESENTATION BY DR. DYLAN MOORE REGARDING THE UNIVERSITY OF
HAWAI’I ECONOMIC RESEARCH ORGANIZATION (UHERO) TRC PROJECT
INTERIM FINDINGS:
1. Overview of Analysis
Dr. Dylan Moore presented an in-depth cost-benefit analysis of the Hawaii Residential
Renewable Energy Tax Credit, focusing primarily on behavioral responses, benefit allocation,
and carbon reduction impacts. He noted the central analytical challenge: determining how many
additional households install solar due solely to the tax credit.
2. Assumptions About Additional Installations
• Base assumption: For every 100 households that would install solar without the credit, 45
additional households install because of the credit.
• This assumption is derived from academic literature and elasticity estimates from
mainland studies.
• Dr. Moore noted this figure may be too high for Hawaii, due to the already high
penetration of rooftop solar, removal of the federal credit, and remaining households
likely facing higher barriers.
3. Benefit Distribution
Dr. Moore explained the difference in valuing benefits for:
• Households who would have installed anyway
• Households induced to install because of the credit
Key estimates per dollar of state expenditure:
• $0.56 benefits to households who would have installed anyway
• $0.13 benefits to newly encouraged households
• $0.16 captured by installers via higher prices or wages
Total: $0.84 in local private benefits per dollar spent.
4. Environmental Benefits
• Major benefits arise from reduced carbon emissions.

• Using HECO emissions data and EPA’s social cost of carbon, Dylan estimates $1.70 in
global environmental benefits for each $1 spent.
• Most of these benefits accrue outside Hawaii (global climate impacts).
• Under Hawaii-only benefit accounting, benefits are closer to $0.71 per dollar.
• If HECO successfully decarbonizes the grid, emissions-related benefits drop (e.g., to
~$0.97).
5. Other Externalities
• Local air quality benefits appear relatively small due to geographic dispersion of
emissions.
• Impacts on consumer rates or HECO profits could create additional costs (estimated
~$0.13 per dollar spent).
• Discussion of how similar benefit/cost dynamics appear in other credits (e.g., Earned
Income Tax Credit, film tax credits).
6. Sensitivity and Elasticity Scenarios
Dr. Moore displayed graphs illustrating how assumed demand elasticity affects total benefits.
Key point: higher behavioral response → more global benefits, while lower response →
resembles a direct transfer to residents.
Estimated realistic range may be closer to 20 additional installations per 100 baseline installers,
rather than 45.
7. Broader Policy Context
• Tax credits should be compared against alternative policies pursuing the same objective
(e.g., utility-scale solar, potential regulatory or tax changes).
• Some alternatives may offer greater carbon-reduction efficiency.
• Issues of who benefits (higher-income households tend to capture most solar credit
benefits).
