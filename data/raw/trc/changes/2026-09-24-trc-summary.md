# TRC update, 2026-09-24: COST told the Commission Hawaii's regressive GET is "offset" by its progressive income tax — the opposite of the Primer's framing — while Knox's data shows only 2.48% of TAT revenue actually funds tourism, versus 72% to the general fund

The Commission's September 18 meeting featured two outside presentations — the Council on State Taxation (COST) on Hawaii's overall tax structure and Dr. John Knox on the TAT and tourism — neither of which produced a vote or Commission position, but both put new numbers and a competing regressivity narrative on the record that bear directly on Appleseed's published work.

## What was discussed

**COST: "A National Perspective on Hawaii's State and Local Taxes."** Karl Frieden (VP & General Counsel) and Dylan Waits (Senior Tax Counsel) of COST — a 450-member trade association that "does not take a position on a state's overall level of taxation or on tax rates" — walked the Commission through a comparative review. Key points, per the slides and the minutes' summary:
- Hawaii businesses paid **$6.3 billion** in state and local taxes in FY24 (up 9.3% from FY23), equal to **41.6%** of all Hawaii state/local tax revenue.
- Hawaii's GET rate (4.5%, i.e., 4.0% state plus 0.5% in most counties) is *below* the U.S. average state/local sales tax rate (7.5%), but the base is unusually broad — similar only to South Dakota and New Mexico — and, because Hawaii exempts very few business inputs, GET "pyramids": the same value gets taxed multiple times moving through the supply chain, pushing effective rates above the statutory 4–4.5%.
- COST recommended Hawaii **extend the corporate filing deadline** beyond the current schedule, **move from 3-factor to single-sales-factor apportionment** for the corporate income tax (Hawaii is now a minority-position state on this), **lengthen its appeal window from 30 to 90 days**, and **improve federal-audit-change conformity** — calling these changes "revenue-neutral."
- COST explicitly told the Commission Hawaii does **not** need a separate digital advertising tax (a bill, H.B. 1458, was introduced in 2026) because the GET's broad base already reaches digital ad sales.
- Commissioners asked about effective rates after pyramiding, equity and competitiveness implications, Hawaii's economic-structure/tax-structure relationship, and treatment of flow-through entities; COST "responded with detailed analysis of pyramiding studies, national comparisons, and Hawaii's unusual structural profile." No commissioner is named in these exchanges. Chair White reminded presenters of time constraints given the agenda.

**Dr. John Knox: "The TAT and Tourism 'Competitiveness'."** Knox presented Part 1 of a planned two-part historical review of TAT/GET rate changes and tourism outcomes:
- Hawaii's combined lodging tax burden — state and municipal hotel taxes plus sales taxes — is **18.71%**, the **6th highest** of the 100 U.S. cities studied (national range: 8% to 19.34%); only Connecticut and DC ranked slightly higher on a pre-Green-Fee state basis last year.
- Despite the high rate, Knox found **no strong correlation** between historical TAT/GET rate changes and most tourism metrics: visitor arrivals and average visitor census show a *positive* long-term correlation with tax increases (i.e., increases didn't precede declines); length of stay shows only a weak correlation; real visitor spending is near-zero correlated; daily spending's historically negative correlation reversed after the pandemic.
- Where visitors do react is cost broadly: "too expensive" remains the top reason surveyed visitors give for not planning a return trip, and in the most recent quarter (2026-Q1) 51.6% of U.S. West visitors and 47.4% of U.S. East visitors cited high cost of travel as a reason for choosing other destinations.
- On revenue use, Knox reported Hawaii allocates only **2.48%** of lodging-tax revenue to tourism-related purposes (marketing, management, infrastructure) — **3rd lowest** of the 100 cities, versus a 41% national average — while **72.41%** goes to the general fund (14th highest) and total non-tourism uses reach **97.52%** (3rd highest). The remaining 25.05% ("Other," 17th highest) is mostly Honolulu's rail/mass-transit fund.
- Knox's recommendations: track *all* tourism-related revenue (including fees), and consider floor/ceiling percentage guidelines for how TAT dollars are allocated across marketing, management, infrastructure and general use. He flagged this would be politically difficult.
- Commission discussion (no individual commissioners named) covered variation in tax impact across hotel market segments, reliability of visitor-arrival-form data, and possible reasons average daily spend has risen; Knox noted average party size has slightly increased and flagged methodological concerns with electronic arrival forms.

**Procedural items.** The September 1 final minutes were approved without changes to what was already reported in last week's summary (the UHERO contract vote, Moore's elasticity walk-back, etc.) — the finalized text matches the draft. The Commission agreed to invite UHERO for "a brief status update related to the Commission's interim report timeline" at a future meeting, and deferred scheduling beyond September 29 until that meeting.

## Fairness and regressivity

The sharpest fairness-relevant moment is COST's own framing, delivered to the Commission and recorded in the minutes: Hawaii's heavy reliance on the GET creates a "regressivity impact," but that is "offset by graduated rate personal income and corporate income taxes," leaving Hawaii "in the middle of the states in ITEP's progressivity/regressivity study." That is a materially softer characterization than the Budget Primer's own use of ITEP data, which states plainly that low- and middle-income Hawaii families pay a larger share of income in state and local taxes than wealthy families, "mostly due to the GET." COST is not disputing the ITEP numbers — it's arguing the income tax's progressivity should be read as counterbalancing them. This is exactly the argument a business association would raise against proposals (like a millionaire's tax) that lean on the "GET is regressive" case, since it recasts the current income-tax structure as already doing corrective work rather than needing to do more.

Knox's TAT findings are a different kind of equity point: TAT is paid overwhelmingly by non-residents, so its regressivity for Hawaii residents is limited, but the allocation data is a distributional finding in its own right — money collected under the banner of "resource management, climate resiliency, and tourism promotion" (as the Primer describes it) is overwhelmingly landing in the general fund instead, a gap between the tax's public rationale and its actual use.

## Implications for published work

- **Budget Primer**: The TAT card (`taxes.cards.tat.bullets`) says the TAT "supports the general fund and special funds for tourism promotion, resource management, climate resiliency and economic development" — true as a list of eligible uses, but Knox's numbers (2.48% to tourism-related uses, 72.41% to the general fund) show the overwhelming majority goes to the general fund, not the named special funds. Worth a revision or footnote if the Primer wants to describe actual allocation rather than statutory purpose. Separately, COST's competing regressivity framing is worth noting as an active counter-narrative circulating at the Commission the Primer's GET/whopays framing may need to be prepared to rebut.
- **RETITC analysis**: No new RETITC-specific content this cycle; the only link is the Commission's plan to have UHERO give a status update on the interim report timeline at a future meeting — worth tracking for a firm date on the final report.
- **Millionaire's tax report**: COST's "offset by graduated income taxes... middle of the states" line is the most directly relevant new material — it's the shape of argument likely to surface in Legislature testimony against raising top rates further, now on the record with this Commission. Single-sales-factor apportionment was also raised for the second time in three weeks (Chair White's own write-up on Sept 1, now COST's slide deck), with "general commissioner support" and no objections either time — a corporate-tax change worth flagging as more likely than before, even without a vote yet.
