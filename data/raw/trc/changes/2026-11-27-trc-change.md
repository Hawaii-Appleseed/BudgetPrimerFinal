1. Summary of Recommendation
This proposal recommends that the Legislature convert the residential conveyance tax under
Hawaii Revised Statutes (HRS) § 247-2 from its current flat-rate, “cliff” structure to a marginal
rate structure, designed for one purpose: to reduce and make fairer the conveyance tax paid on the
purchase of a home by Hawaii owner-occupants. While the Conveyance Tax is customarily a
“Seller” expense, it could translate to a savings for Buyers.
The recommended measure differs from recent marginal-rate bills (H.B. 1410, H.D. 2 (2025) and
S.B. 3028, S.D. 2, H.D. 2 (2026)) in two deliberate respects:
• No new earmarks. The measure does not amend HRS § 247-7 or direct any conveyance
tax revenue to new or existing special funds. It is a rate-structure bill only.
• Homeowner-first rate design. The owner-occupant schedule is built so that the tax on an
owner-occupied home is lower than or equal to the tax under current law at every price
below approximately $32.6 million. Unlike the prior bills, which raised the tax on owner-
occupants above roughly $1.35 million (H.B. 1410) or $1.8 million (S.B. 3028), no owner-
occupant in that range would pay more than today.
Core elements:
a) a marginal owner-occupant schedule under § 247-2(a)(1); and
b) proposed structure for non-owner-occupant schedule.
A Department of Taxation revenue estimate is required to review the tax revenue feasibility
of this proposal and the schedules should be adjusted accordingly.

Because the full price is taxed at the bracket rate, the structure produces sharp jumps that fall most
heavily on ordinary local purchases near Hawaii's typical home prices:
• A local family buying a home for $1,000,001 pays $3,000 — $1,000 more than a buyer at
$999,999, which costs $2,000.
• At $1.1 million, an owner-occupant pays $3,300 — an effective rate 50% higher than a
purchase just under $1 million.
• Because the brackets are not indexed, ordinary appreciation steadily moves moderately
priced homes into higher brackets, raising the tax on homeowners without any legislative
action.
The cliff also creates an incentive to manipulate reported consideration around thresholds (e.g.,
shifting value into personal property or credits) and distorts pricing near $600,000, $1 million, and
$2 million, the very range where most local owner-occupant purchases occur.

6. Revenue Disposition — No New Earmarks
This proposal intentionally leaves HRS § 247-7 unamended. Conveyance tax collections would
continue to be distributed under existing law (currently, the lesser of 10% or $5,100,000 to the
land conservation fund and the lesser of 50% or $38,000,000 to the rental housing revolving
fund, with the balance to the general fund). No new special fund is created and no existing
allocation is increased.
By contrast, H.B. 1410 HD2 would have restructured § 247-7 to fund a new supportive housing
special fund and the dwelling unit revolving fund, and S.B. 3028 HD2 would have created a
Hawaiian home lands infrastructure and housing special fund and a transit-oriented development
infrastructure subaccount, while reducing the existing land conservation and rental housing
allocations.
Reasons for a rate-only bill
• Clarity of purpose. The measure is justified by fairness to homeowners, not by the
revenue it raises. Keeping it free of allocation provisions avoids tying homeowner relief
to the competing interests of fund beneficiaries.
• Legislative control of spending. Any net revenue change flows through the regular
appropriations process, a point raised in testimony on S.B. 3028 cautioning against
routing special funding through the conveyance tax.
• Single-subject drafting. A narrow bill limited to § 247-2 reduces the risk of challenge
under article III, section 14 of the Hawaii Constitution and is easier to move through
committees.
• Volatility. Conveyance tax collections rise and fall with the real estate market and are
poorly suited to fund programs that need stable annual support.

Objective:
Modernize Hawai‘i’s corporate income tax to better address the modern global economy and
ensure its robustness in the face of federal changes.
Proposed Recommendation:
Further study and consider options for modernizing and bolstering Hawai‘i’s corporate
income tax, including a transition to mandatory worldwide combined reporting as well as the
alternatives described below.
Narrative:
The legislature is aware of this topic and has requested the Department to study the issue
further and develop a revenue estimate for it.1 The Commission believes its research and
description of the issues will add to the Department’s eventual report and to the general
discourse. This narrative does not represent a complete or comprehensive
recommendation. Rather, this is a recommendation to further study the topic.
As described above (note: this refers to the report’s single sales factor writeup), state
corporate income taxes, including Hawai‘i’s, use apportionment formulas to apportion (split
up) taxpayers’ taxable income among the states. This calculation involves two things, the
first is the apportionment formula itself and the second is the taxable income that is split up
by the apportionment formula. The Commission’s earlier recommendation, to transition to
single sales factor apportionment, concerned the actual apportionment formula. This
recommendation, on the other hand, considers the universe of taxable income that is
exposed to the apportionment formula.
To determine this pot of income, Hawai‘i’s corporate income tax utilizes water’s edge
combined reporting.2 This means that only the income of domestic entities is included and
exposed to Hawai‘i’s apportionment factor to determine Hawai‘i’s share. Transitioning to a

worldwide combined reporting method would change that, and the income of foreign
affiliates would go into the pot of income. Additionally, the attributes of those foreign
affiliates would be included in the Hawai‘i apportionment formula. So, not only would the
income from foreign affiliates be included in the pot of income, but the attributes of those
foreign affiliates, their sales, payroll, and property, would be included in the three factors of
the Hawai‘i apportionment formula. Under current law, the factors only include the
attributes of the domestic affiliates, similar to how the pot of taxable income only includes
the income of the domestic affiliates.
Mandatory worldwide combined reporting is constitutional. However, it is not fully used by
any states currently.3 The cases holding mandatory worldwide combined reporting
constitutional are Container Corporation,4 and Barclays Bank.5 At the time Container was
decided, more than 10 states required worldwide combined reporting.6 Despite this, there

This report examines a series of tax reforms, some of which would raise revenue and others
reduce it. A tax cut has to be paid for somehow, and a tax increase pays for something, so a
proposal that is silent about either side of the ledger is incomplete. Chapter 9 first estimates,
for each major tax, what taxpayers give up for every dollar the state collects after a small
increase (Table 9.1). It then estimates seven revenue-neutral combinations of changes to the
general excise tax (GET), income tax, food/excise tax credit, transient accommodations tax
and property tax (section 9.7), among them an exemption of groceries from the GET. Two
more, which change how capital gains are taxed, are in Appendix I. We call the second change
in each pair the financing change, or offset.
Except for user or benefit charges (a park entrance fee, say, that pays for the park), the source of
tax revenue does not ordinarily determine how it is spent. If the aim is to do the most good for
residents, taking account of the cost, there is no reason the money raised by taxing groceries
has to be spent on grocery shoppers, or the money raised from hotel rooms on visitors. Thus,
we consider each tax together with what it pays for. In each case we ask who would gain, who
would lose, and how people’s responses would change those gains and losses, as well as the
revenue available to the state. Since the answers often depend on assumptions, we explain
what we have assumed and why it matters wherever they do. UHERO did the analysis and
estimates in this report; what to recommend is for the Commission to decide. Unless a table
or figure gives an earlier date, the law described is the law as of November 20, 2026.

It matters which tax raises the revenue, because different taxes fall on different people and
behavior changes when taxes change. When the state taxes people, their value to the tax
system can fall: they might work less, earn less or turn down a raise, either by changing how
much they actually earn or by relabeling their income. They might even leave the tax system
entirely, by moving away or leaving the labor force.3
By contrast, the supply of land is fixed.4 Taxing land can lower its market value because buyers
account for the taxes they will pay, but it does not reduce the amount of land. Buildings are
different: taxing new construction and improvements can discourage investment. In chapter
9, a 1 percent increase in all county property tax rates loses about 2 percent of its revenue to
changed behavior, and it lowers owners’ property values by about $759 million.5 The model
holds land fixed and lets owners build and maintain a little less when the tax rises, which
accounts for the lost revenue.
This report pays attention to two features of a tax base (whatever the tax is levied on). The
first is how responsive it is: how easily it can shrink, move or hide. The second is its spillovers,
the effects of a tax on people other than the taxpayer, from the workers whose pay depends on
a business to the collections of other taxes. The cost of raising a dollar depends on both. It is
higher when the base can move or hide, and higher again when the response spills over onto
other people.6 In chapter 9’s estimates for small tax increases, taxpayers give up about $1.03
for each net dollar raised through the GET, $1.02 through the property tax, $1.42 through all
income-tax rates and $2.02 through the top rate (Table 9.1). If no household left the state in
response, the two income-tax figures would be $1.28 and $1.42.7
