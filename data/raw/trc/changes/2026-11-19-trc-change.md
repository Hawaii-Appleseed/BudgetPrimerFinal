Presentation Summary
Dr. Lacoste explained that the TAT is Hawaii’s third-largest source of tax revenue and reviewed
research intended to estimate how changes in TAT rates affect tax collections and visitor
behavior. Using economic modeling and a synthetic-control methodology that compares Hawai‘i
with tourism-intensive jurisdictions elsewhere in the United States and Guam, the study seeks to
estimate the revenue effects of TAT increases.
Key points from the presentation included:
• Historical TAT increases generally resulted in higher TAT revenue.
• Tourist behavioral responses appear to reduce, but not eliminate, the revenue gains from
TAT increases.
• The analysis suggests Hawai‘i likely remains below the revenue-maximizing TAT rate
when examining TAT collections alone.
• When broader impacts on General Excise Tax (GET) collections and economic activity
are considered, revenue effects become less certain.
• TAT increases may affect visitor spending patterns and indirectly influence sectors such
as recreation, attractions, dining, and wages.
Dr. Lacoste emphasized that policymakers should evaluate TAT changes within the context of
the entire tax system rather than viewing the tax in isolation.

State 1031 Clawback: How It Works
The problem states are solving
IRC §1031 lets a taxpayer defer federal gain by exchanging real property for like-kind
property, and most states follow that deferral. But a state generally taxes nonresidents only
on income sourced to that state. If an investor exchanges property in State A for property in
State B and later sells the State B property in a taxable sale, State A could lose its chance to
tax the gain that built up while the investor owned property there. A clawback rule closes
that gap. The relinquished-property state treats the deferred gain as still belonging to it and
taxes it when the deferral ends.
How it works
1. The taxpayer does a §1031 exchange out of State A into replacement property in
another state.
2. The state tracks the deferred gain from the original exchange, often through an
annual information return.
3. If the replacement property is later sold in a taxable transaction, State A taxes the
deferred gain as State A-source income, even if the taxpayer no longer lives or owns
property there.
4. If the taxpayer exchanges again under §1031, the deferral generally continues and
no tax is due yet.
Illustration (hypothetical numbers) – if Hawaii adopted a 1031 Clawback
An investor owns a Hawaii rental with a $400,000 basis and a $1.5 million value, so $1.1
million of gain is deferred. They exchange into a Texas property, then later sell it outright for
$1.8 million. The federal gain is $1.4 million. Hawaii would tax the $1.1 million of deferred
gain, not the $300,000 of later appreciation, which arose in Texas.
Similarly, if the original property was not in Hawaii, the gain recognized in Hawaii would be
limited to the appreciation of the Hawaii property only. Keeping the same facts as in the
above example but adding in the investor originally had a California property with a
$400,000 basis and $900,000 value, so $500,000 gain is deferred. They exchanged into a
Hawaii rental worth the same $900,000 value and later exchanged the Hawaii property into
a Texas property when the value of the Hawaii property was $1.5 million. The Texas
property was later sold outright for $1.8 million. The federal gain is $1.4 million. Of the
$1.4 million, $600,000 of gain would be sourced to Hawaii ($1.5 million - $900,000).

Which states have clawbacks
California, Oregon, Massachusetts, and Montana are the four consistently named.
California uses Form FTB 3840, authorized by R&TC §18032 for individuals and §24953 for
corporations. I couldn’t find annual required filings for the other states but I would suggest
an annual filing for Hawaii because it is easier to track and reminds the taxpayer of the
clawback. If a taxpayer changes CPAs, this could get lost without an annual filing
requirement.
California form specifics that I would propose Hawaii follow:
• The annual filing duty continues until the deferred California gain is recognized,
even if the taxpayer otherwise has no California filing requirement.
• It also continues when the out-of-state replacement property is itself exchanged
again.
Planning considerations
• Keep deferring. Another like-kind exchange preserves the deferral, and exchanging
back into property in the original state resets the issue.
• Watch for double tax. A taxpayer resident in the replacement state may be taxed on
the full gain there and on the deferred gain by the relinquished-property state. A
credit for taxes paid to other states may help.
• Compliance. I think any 1031 exchange done with Hawaii property should trigger a
letter to the taxpayer informing them of the clawback provisions. California
currently does this for enforcement purposes.
• Death and other triggers. Similar to federal step up at date of death provisions, I
would propose that Hawaii adopt the same step up provisions if the taxpayer owner
dies.
California, Montana, and Oregon laws
See attached laws for California, Montana, and Oregon. I could only find California law
that requires the form to be filed that tracks the 1031 gain deferral so it seems that their
rules are laid out in their Form 3840 so those instructions are attached for reference.
Between Oregon and Montana, I think the Montana laws would be ideal to follow since they
are much more detailed and easier to follow with the examples.

Section 317.327. Modification of taxable income when deferred gain is recognized
as result of out-of-state disposition of property; rules
(1) If gain is deferred upon the voluntary or involuntary disposition of property in an exchange that qualifies for deferral

California Code, Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code § 18032, Information return
California Code
CALIFORNIA REVENUE AND TAXATION CODE
Division 2. OTHER TAXES (§§ 6001 to 61050)
Part 10. PERSONAL INCOME TAX (§§ 17001 to 18181)
Chapter 13. GAIN OR LOSS ON DISPOSITION OF PROPERTY (§§ 18031 to 18045)
Section 18032. Information return
(a) If gain or loss from the exchange of property in this state of a taxpayer is not recognized under this part because of
Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code, relating to exchange of property held for productive use or investment, for a
taxable year and the property acquired in that exchange is located outside of this state, the taxpayer shall file an
information return with the Franchise Tax Board for the taxable year of the exchange and for each subsequent taxable
year in which the gain or loss from that exchange has not been recognized, in the form and manner prescribed by the
Franchise Tax Board.
(b) If a taxpayer fails to file an information return required pursuant to subdivision (a), and fails to file a return required
under Part 10.2 (commencing with Section 18401), the Franchise Tax Board may make an estimate of the net income,
from any available information, including the amount of gain described in subdivision (a), and may propose to assess
the amount of tax, interest, and penalties due in the same manner as Section 19087.
(c) Chapter 3.5 (commencing with Section 11340) of Part 1 of Division 3 of Title 2 of the Government Code shall not
apply to any standard, criterion, procedure, determination, rule, notice, or guideline established or issued by the
Franchise Tax Board pursuant to this section.
(d) This section shall apply to exchanges of property that occur in taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 2014.
Added by Stats 2013 ch 26 (AB 92),s 4, eff. 6/27/2013.

Rule 42.2.308. NONRESIDENT CALCULATION OF MONTANA SOURCE INCOME
REALIZED AND RECOGNIZED WHEN MONTANA PROPERTY IS RELINQUISHED AS
PART OF A SECTION 1031 EXCHANGE
(1) Gain realized on the transfer of Montana real or tangible personal property retains its Montana source income
character and must be reported if and when the gain is recognized for federal income tax purposes.
(2) When the Montana property is relinquished in a section 1031 exchange for like-kind property, the gain realized as
shown on the taxpayer's U.S. Treasury Form 8824 is generally the amount of Montana source income. If, however, the
Montana property was itself acquired in a prior like-kind exchange as replacement property for property located outside
the state, Montana source income does not include the gain attributable to the out-of-state property that has been
deferred from the prior exchange as shown on the taxpayer's U.S. Treasury Form 8824 for that exchange. The following
examples illustrate how the Montana source income may be calculated in different situations:
(a) Example 1 - A nonresident relinquishes unimproved Montana land with a fair market value of $100,000 and an
adjusted tax basis of $10,000 in exchange for Wyoming property with a fair market value of $90,000 and $10,000
cash, and incurs exchange expenses of $2,000. The $88,000 realized gain reported on the taxpayer's Form 8824 is
Montana source income, of which $8,000 is recognized and reportable in the year of sale and $80,000 is deferred
and must be reported if and when the gain is recognized for federal income tax purposes:

(a) Example 1 - Same facts as in (2)(a). In a later tax year, the Wyoming replacement property is sold for $150,000,
the taxpayer reporting taxable gain of $140,000 on their federal income tax return. The nonresident's $80,000 of
Montana source income realized on the Montana property exchange that was deferred has been recognized for
federal income tax purposes and must be reported as Montana source income when the nonresident files the
Montana individual income tax return required in 15-30-2104, MCA.
(b) Example 2 - Same facts as in (3)(a), except the Wyoming replacement property is sold for $60,000 and $50,000
of taxable gain is reported on the taxpayer's federal income tax return. While $80,000 of Montana source income
was realized but deferred on relinquishment of the Montana property, only $50,000 was recognized for federal
income tax purposes. The nonresident must report the $50,000 of Montana source income recognized when the
nonresident files the Montana individual income tax return required in 15-30-2104, MCA.
(c) Example 3 - Same facts as in (3)(a), except improvements with a cost of $75,000 are erected on the Wyoming
property and depreciation deductions of $15,000 are claimed with respect to those improvements before the
improved Wyoming property is sold for $150,000. On the date of sale the fair market value of the improvements,
which have an adjusted basis of $60,000, is $70,000, and the fair market value of the Wyoming property acquired
in the exchange, which has an adjusted basis of $10,000, is $80,000. Of the $80,000 of deferred Montana source
income realized on relinquishment of the Montana property, $70,000 has been recognized for federal income tax
purposes and must be reported when the nonresident files the Montana individual income tax required in 15-30-
2104, MCA.
(d) Example 4 - Same facts as in (2)(c). The Wyoming replacement property is sold for $150,000. The $30,000
deferred Montana source income realized on relinquishment of the Montana property has been recognized for
federal income tax purposes and must be reported when the nonresident files the Montana individual income tax
return required in 15-30-2104, MCA.
