# TRC update, 2026-09-03: UHERO's first RETITC scorecard lands, and it's bad news for the credit

The headline: UHERO's Dylan Moore told the Commission on September 1 that Hawaii's residential solar tax credit (RETITC) returns just **$0.71 to Hawaiʻi residents for every $1 of net state cost** — worse than simply mailing people a check — and that most of what residents do get is a transfer to households who would have installed solar anyway, not new activity the credit caused.

## What was discussed

**August 11 minutes (routine business + one real decision).** The Commission (Chair Jonathan White, Vice-Chair Judith Chock, Jessica Enos, Mark Fukunaga, Stacey Katakura, Jeffrey Ueoka, Jon Yasuda) formally approved the UHERO consulting arrangement: Dr. Dylan Moore (joined in the fall by Dr. Nicolas Lacoste) will study Hawaii's "tax competitiveness" — which taxes drive people or businesses to leave, which are "easy to escape" versus hard, and what a revenue-neutral alternative tax structure could look like. Dr. Karacaovali put the budget at roughly **$150,000**. Chair White pressed on whether the real priority was revenue neutrality or cutting the top income tax / eliminating the corporate tax; Fukunaga clarified the proposal's list is possibilities to explore, not a predetermined target — revenue neutrality is the stated objective.

Substantive topics the Commission worked through:
- **Retirement-income taxation** (their stated primary focus): Yasuda raised poor Schedule J compliance; Enos said the Commission can't currently measure the fiscal impact of excluding pension income because the data isn't collected, and suggested revising the form; Katakura confirmed with Karacaovali that recommending tax-form changes is within TRC's purview. White volunteered to draft form-change language for the next meeting.
- **Single Sales Factor apportionment** for the corporate income tax: Enos and Fukunaga both argued it would raise "marginally more" revenue for Hawaii without much hurting local companies, since few corporations are Hawaii-headquartered. White noted 34 states use SSF and flagged HRS §235-8, which lets a taxpayer challenge the statutory apportionment formula as unfair.
- **Framework for evaluating tax credits/exemptions**: raised but not resolved — Dr. John Knox asked whether the Commission had considered "endemic vs. intrinsic" logic models; no follow-up recorded.
- New topics queued for future meetings: IRC §1031 exchange clawbacks (Enos), conveyance-tax controlling-interest provisions (White), and matching the state estate tax to the federal exemption — the last raised because Ueoka got a call from **Senator Hashimoto** asking the Commission to look at it.
- The Commission voted to **accelerate to biweekly meetings**.

**September 1 presentation — the substantive item.** This is Moore's first interim deliverable under the new UHERO contract, and it's a full cost-benefit teardown of the Renewable Energy Technologies Income Tax Credit (RETITC), covering only the residential/individual side for now. In 2022, **$106 million** was claimed under the credit; **$49 million** of that was on corporate returns, leaving roughly **$57 million** claimed by households (the residential piece analyzed here).

Moore's method: treat the credit as spending that happens to run through the tax code, and ask where every dollar of net state cost actually ends up, benchmarked against $1.00 — the value of just cutting residents a check.

The accounting, per $1 of net state cost (central estimate, using a mainland-borrowed demand elasticity of 1.14, since Hawaii-specific data isn't available):
- **$0.56** — households that would have installed solar with no credit at all; this money buys no new activity, it's a straight transfer.
- **$0.13** — value actually captured by the ~45 households (of every 145 claimants) the credit *did* convince to install; the marginal buyer, by definition, was nearly indifferent, so they value the credit at close to nothing.
- **$0.16** — captured by solar installers as higher prices/profits when the credit pushes demand up.
- **−$0.14** — cost imposed on other ratepayers and HECO, as lost utility revenue gets recovered through rate hikes on everyone's bill, including non-claimants.
- **Hawaiʻi residents, total: $0.71.**
- **$1.70** — global climate benefit (avoided oil-burning), using the EPA social cost of carbon. Most of this accrues to people outside Hawaii, since climate change is global.
- **Everything, total: $2.41.**

So the credit only "pays for itself" against the $1 cash benchmark once you count benefits to the entire planet; counted for Hawaii residents alone, it's a loser. Moore flagged the climate figure as sensitive to how much of HECO's decarbonization plan actually gets delivered — if HECO hits 60% of its target, the displaced power is cleaner and the climate benefit itself falls (from $1.70 toward $0.97). He also noted a real but "small" local-air-quality benefit he couldn't reliably size, and pointed out that on the mainland, utility-scale solar subsidies return 2–3x more per dollar than residential ones — though no Hawaii-specific study of that alternative exists yet.

## Fairness and regressivity

This is the part Appleseed will want to lead with: **UHERO's own slide is titled "The credit flows mostly to higher-income households."** Moore states that claimants cluster at median income and above, because claiming RETITC requires owning a roof and having enough tax liability to use a nonrefundable credit — which structurally excludes renters and lower-income households. Meanwhile the cost side — rate hikes to cover HECO's lost revenue — is spread across *all* ratepayers, including renters and low-income households who could never claim the credit in the first place. That's a textbook regressive transfer: benefits concentrated among higher-income homeowners, costs socialized across the full ratepayer base regardless of income.

## Implications for Appleseed's published work

- **RETITC analysis**: this is a significant, independent corroboration from a state-commissioned economist. If Appleseed's existing RETITC piece argues the credit is poorly targeted and mostly subsidizes people who'd have gone solar anyway, Moore's numbers give it hard, citable backing — $0.56 of every dollar to non-additional installs, another $0.16 captured by installers, only $0.13 of value reaching the households the credit actually swayed, and a resident-only return ($0.71) below the cash-mailing benchmark. The regressive-incidence finding (claimants skew median-income-and-up, costs spread to all ratepayers) is worth pulling directly into any update.
- **Budget Primer**: gives a concrete, sourced number for the size of the credit ($106M claimed in 2022, $49M corporate / ~$57M residential) plus an authoritative cost-benefit framing to cite if the Primer discusses RETITC as a budget line or tax expenditure.
- **Millionaire's tax report**: no direct connection in this packet. The UHERO tax-competitiveness engagement will eventually examine "lowering the top income tax" as one scenario, per White's exchange with Fukunaga on August 11, so future TRC output is worth watching for relevance — but nothing here bears on it yet.
