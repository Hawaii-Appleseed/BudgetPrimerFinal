Conveyance Tax Controlling Interest:
Objective:
To increase the equity and efficiency of Hawai‘i’s Conveyance Tax by adding a provision that will
trigger the tax upon the transfer of entities holding interests in real property.
Proposed Recommendation:
Add a provision that would trigger the conveyance tax if a buyer purchases a controlling share of a
company or trust that owns real estate in the state.
On Effectiveness:
One issue considered by the Commission was the possibility that a conveyance tax applied to
controlling interests would be only marginally more difficult to avoid than the current conveyance
tax. This is a valid concern. Every law creates a way around itself. However, it is difficult to believe
that the other seventeen states with controlling interest provisions are just being walked all over. The
Commission’s recommendation below is built from the practices of several states’ taxes and should
ensure broad but not perfect application.
Nonetheless, the effectiveness of states’ controlling interest provisions is difficult to judge. A 2018
proposal from New York proposed to expand the state’s controlling interest provision. The expansion
would have applied the conveyance tax to all transfers of an interest in an entity that held New York
real estate if real estate comprised fifty percent or more of the entity’s total value after the transfer.
In other words, the requirement of a transfer of fifty percent or more of the voting shares of an entity
would be gone for these entities. The change was estimated to produce an additional $5 million per
year. The point of this is not to recommend it for Hawai‘i. The point is only to highlight that the
controlling interest provision must be taxing at least some transfers of controlling interests if there is
a proposal to expand it in this way that is also estimated to raise revenue.1
There will be tax planning; some of it successful.2 The exact amount is impossible to predict.
Regardless, the Commission believes improvement of horizontal equity justifies imposing a tax on
the transfer of a controlling interest in real property.
Narrative and Detail:
Hawai‘i’s conveyance tax applies to the transfer of real property interests.3 However, the tax does not
apply to the transfer of an interest in an entity that itself owns a real property interest. This is a
violation of horizontal equity. The Commission believes the conveyance tax should apply to all
transfers of real property whether the interest is held by an individual or a legal entity.
Seventeen jurisdictions agree and apply a controlling interest provision within their conveyance

straightforward of any state.6 New Jersey’s law contains less than 1,200 words in total.
The law contains the standard and near universal definition of a “controlling interest.” Under the

A Previous Hawai‘i Proposal and Specific Commission Recommendations:
In 2026, the Legislature considered H.B. 1918.49 H.B. 1918 proposed to impose tax on transfers of
controlling interests generally, as did all of the Hawai‘i bills the Commission surveyed. The
Commission considers this bill a great starting point and suggests a few amendments below.
H.B. 1918 defined “controlling interest” for corporations, and separately, for partnerships,
associations, trusts, and other entities. For corporations, a controlling interest is fifty percent or more
of the total combined voting power of all classes of the corporation’s stock, or, fifty percent or more
of the capital, profits, or beneficial interest in the voting stock of the corporation. For partnerships,
associations, trusts, and other entities, a controlling interest is fifty percent or more of the capital,
profits, or beneficial interest in the partnership, association, trust, or other entity. This language is
standard language and shared across many jurisdictions’ controlling interest provisions.50 The
Commission recommends this language be maintained.
H.B. 1918 would have applied the conveyance tax to transfers that result in (1) the transfer of a
controlling interest in an entity with an interest in real property located in the Hawai‘i for valuable
consideration; and (2) the transfer of control or ownership of the real property located in Hawai‘i.
The proposed imposition would have applied to a transfer or series of transfers “by any person or
entity acting alone or in concert, within any twelve-month period.” The bill provided that persons are
acting in concert when there is a relationship between them such that one person influences or
controls the actions of another through common ownership. The bill further provides that persons
are only considered to be acting in concert when the way in which the transfer is negotiated and
consummated supports that they are acting in concert.51
These words are very similar to language used in other states’ laws, for example New York, but the
meaning is different here. In other states, these two clauses are two independent ways in which
persons can be found to be acting in concert. In H.B. 1918, the conjunctive “and” is used between
these two clauses. This means the second clause would restrict the application of the first. This
means that to be acting in concert the parties would have to have a relationship such that one
influences the other through common ownership and the way in which the transfer is negotiated and
consummated would have to support that they are acting in concert.

Objective:
Simplify Hawai‘i’s corporate income tax by simplifying the apportionment formula. Align
Hawai‘i’s corporate income tax system with that of other states. The recommended
adjustment will likely raise revenue.
Proposed Recommendation:
Adopt a corporate income tax apportionment formula based on a single factor reflecting
sales in Hawai‘i. Make a conforming amendment to the alternative apportionment
provision.
Narrative:
Currently, Hawai‘i’s corporate income tax system uses an equally weighted three-factor
apportionment formula to apportion taxpayer income between the states.1 This method
was the original method under the Uniform Division of Income for Tax Purposes Act2 but
has been replaced by a single factor reflecting sales (singles sales factor) in most states.3
The single-sales factor was ruled constitutional by the United States Supreme Court in
1978.4
Apportioning corporate net income using a single sales factor apportionment is more
efficient and competitive. The single sales factor encourages investment in employees and
plant and equipment in the state compared to the current three-factor formula.5
The current three-factor formula takes a combination of sales, property, and payroll into
account when apportioning net income to Hawai‘i. As a result, a corporate taxpayer with
increasing employees and assets in Hawai‘i will face an increasing tax liability. Another way
to say this is that the three-factor formula places a tax on jobs and buildings in the state.6
Under a single sales factor system, the share of income Hawai‘i taxes will only increase as
a result of an increase in sales sourced to Hawai‘i relative to total nationwide sales. The
share will not increase due to an increase in employees or property in the state. Thus, one
potential impediment to local expansion is removed.

Options
There are several options that do not involve full or immediate adoption of single sales
factor apportionment. We describe several here.
Gradual transition
One option is to gradually transition into single sales factor apportionment over time. This
can be done by maintaining the property and payroll factors temporarily while increasing
the weighting of the sales factor in the formula each year, then removing the property and
payroll factors completely in a target year. For an example of effective language, see
Tennessee’s statute, which now requires single sales factor, but retains the language from
the transition years.14
Election only
Another option is to allow taxpayers to elect into single sales factor apportionment. An
example of this is Arizona’s current method, which allows a choice between a three-factor
formula with a double-weighted sales factor and the single sales factor method.15 Typically,
once such an election is made, it cannot be revoked for a number of years.
Certain industries
Another option is to require or allow single sales factor apportionment to select industries.
Some statutes or proposals allow the method only for certain industries16 while some
require single sales factor to be used but then provide exceptions to certain industries.17
However, these exceptions are likely based on local conditions and local lobbying, so we
will not provide a common list of excepted industries here.
Double weighted sales factor
Finally, a three-factor with a double-weighted sales factor can be adopted. This option will
increase the emphasis on the sales factor while maintaining a reflection of the payroll and
property in the state. For an example of what a three-factor formula with a double-weighted
sales factor looks like, see Florida’s apportionment factor.18

Historical Correlation Analysis

Use of TAT Revenues

1. How to judge a tax system, and
Hawaiʻi’s today
This report examines a series of tax reforms, some of which would raise revenue and others
reduce it. A proposal that does not say where the revenue comes from, or where it goes, is
incomplete. Chapter 10 describes paired changes to the general excise tax (GET), income tax
and food/excise tax credit, which the final report will estimate. We call the second change in
each pair the financing change, or offset.
Except for user or benefit charges, the source of tax revenue does not ordinarily determine how
it should be spent. We should spend the revenue where it does the most good for residents,
taking account of the cost. That need not be on the people or activities it came from. We
therefore consider each tax together with what it pays for. Who would gain, who would lose,
and how would their responses change those gains and losses and the revenue available to
the state? Where the answers depend on assumptions, we explain what we have assumed and
why it matters.
Take a grocery exemption from the general excise tax (GET), Hawaiʻi’s broad tax on sales.
Before allowing for changes in spending, we estimate that exempting groceries would cost
the state and counties $267.4 million. If stores pass the full tax reduction through to prices,
residents would save $214.1 million and visitors $53.3 million. Suppose residents’ income
taxes go up by enough to make up their $214.1 million. Residents as a group would pay as
much in additional income tax as they save on groceries. Individual households could still gain
or lose, because their grocery purchases and income-tax payments differ. Visitors keep their
$53.3 million and pay no more income tax, so the state and counties are still $53.3 million
short.
One of the pairs in Chapter 10 is a grocery GET exemption with higher income-tax rates to
replace the State General Fund revenue loss. If residents fund that offset, they also pay for
the visitors’ share of the General Fund loss.
Why should it matter which tax raises the revenue? Different taxes fall on different people,
and behavior changes when taxes change. When we tax people, we can reduce their value
to the tax system, because they might work less, they might earn less, or they may be more
likely to turn down a raise, and they can do this either by changing how much they actually
earn or by recharacterizing their income. They might even remove themselves from the tax
system entirely, via migration or labor force participation decisions.1

Hawaiʻi Tax Review Commission · Draft report 1. How to judge a tax system
By contrast, the supply of land is fixed.2 Taxing land can lower its market value because buyers
account for the taxes they will pay, but it does not reduce the amount of land. Buildings are
different: taxing new construction and improvements can discourage investment.
We care about how responsive a tax base (whatever the tax is levied on) is, meaning how
easily it can shrink, move or hide, and we care about spillover effects, meaning the effects a
tax has on people other than the taxpayer, from the workers whose pay depends on a business
to the collections of other taxes. The cost of raising a dollar depends on both: it is higher when
the base can move or hide, and higher again when the response spills over onto other people.3
When people ask whether a state’s tax system is competitive, they are mostly asking how easily
its tax bases can move somewhere else. This shows up in several ways. Residents can move
to another state and take their future earnings with them, and people deciding where to live
and work can choose not to come. Businesses can make new investments in another state, or
report their profits there while the work stays here. Visitors can choose another destination,
or buy at home what they would otherwise have bought here. Owners of wealth can move
it, or themselves, to where it is taxed less. Section 1.3 takes up people, business capital (the
buildings, equipment and other assets businesses use to produce goods and services) and
wealth, section 1.4 takes up visitors, and section 1.6 asks how much taxes matter for these
choices compared with everything else that makes a place attractive.
Not every response is about competitiveness. People can also change when they do things,
such as when they sell an asset and realize a gain, or how they label their income, such as
reporting wages as business profits. These responses shrink the base too, but they usually do

1.3 Responsiveness: whether a tax base can shrink, move or hide
Other things equal, taxes do the least damage when the base cannot easily shrink, move or
hide. Hawaiʻi has land, which cannot move, and at the other end of the range, wealth that
can be hard even to see. People can reduce the amount of activity we tax by working less,
selling fewer homes or investing less. They can move the activity to another state, move there
themselves, or report their income there. Or they can avoid reporting it, report a value below
what it is worth, or describe it as something taxed at a lower rate. The more ways people have
to respond, the less revenue we get from a tax increase, and the greater the cost per dollar
raised to people who change what they do to avoid the tax.
