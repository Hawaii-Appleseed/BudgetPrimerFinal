# TRC update, 2026-09-17: Moore now says his own solar-credit numbers were too generous — the real "induced" rate looks closer to 20 per 100, not 45

The most consequential line in the final September 1 minutes doesn't appear in the presentation slides Appleseed already has: Dr. Dylan Moore told the Commission his headline assumption — that the credit induces 45 new solar installations for every 100 that would have happened anyway — "may be too high for Hawaii," and that "the estimated realistic range may be closer to 20 additional installations per 100 baseline installers." That revision, if it holds, makes RETITC's fairness problem worse than the interim numbers already showed, not better.

## What was discussed

**Public comment from Appleseed itself.** Devin Thomas, Hawai'i Appleseed's Director of Tax & Budget Policy, also representing the Hawai'i Tax Fairness Coalition, addressed the Commission directly — offering Appleseed as a research resource and stating the Coalition's focus on making Hawaii's taxes more equitable. No commissioner response is recorded, but it puts Appleseed on the record with this Commission for the first time in these minutes.

**Legislative outreach, still pending.** Vice-Chair Judith Chock sent a two-page summary of TRC updates to Senate Ways & Means Chair Dela Cruz and Scott Fuji; she'd had no response as of the meeting. Chair Jonathan White then sent his own edited version to House Finance.

**UHERO contract formally voted, not just proposed.** The Memorandum of Agreement is signed by all parties except Chair White (pending only his signature). White then moved to formally engage UHERO, not to exceed $150,000; roll call was unanimous ("aye" from all seven commissioners). This is the actual Commission vote — the August 11 minutes only recorded committee-level sign-off and discussion of the proposal's scope.

**The RETITC presentation, with two things not in the slide deck:**
- DBEDT now has a statutory responsibility, created by the 2026 Legislature, to evaluate tax credits and GET exemptions — commissioners discussed DBEDT's resource constraints in meeting that mandate and floated future UHERO support for DBEDT's evaluations.
- Mark Fukunaga and Chair White discussed building a simpler, non-empirical framework policymakers could apply quickly during session, without a full study like Moore's. Moore agreed a qualitative version could help but stressed its limits without formal analysis.

**Retirement income (Schedule J).** Chair White summarized his written draft on Schedule J under-utilization and its compliance gap with Form N-11 — possible fixes include new data fields, simplified calculations, or a safe-harbor method. Commissioners deferred further discussion until they've read the draft.

**Single Sales Factor apportionment.** White presented a preliminary write-up advocating a move to single-sales-factor for the corporate income tax, citing ~34 other states already there and "prior Hawaii internal modeling" suggesting a revenue gain. Reaction was generally supportive with no objections raised — but no vote was taken.

**Queued for later meetings:** IRC §1031 exchange clawbacks (Jessica Enos, who may take it up after October 15), conveyance-tax controlling-interest provisions (White to prepare background), estate tax modernization, and Voluntary Disclosure Agreement program reform.

## Fairness and regressivity

The elasticity walk-back matters more than it looks. Moore's interim accounting split every credit dollar as $0.56 to households who'd have installed anyway (pure windfall), $0.13 to the marginal households the credit actually convinced, and $0.16 to installers — with the $0.56/$0.13 split resting on the assumption that 45 of every 145 claimants were induced. If the real number is closer to 20 induced per 100 non-induced (roughly 17% induced instead of 31%), a larger share of every credit dollar is windfall to homeowners who were installing regardless, and a smaller share buys the marginal activity the credit is supposed to be for. That pushes the resident-only return below the $0.71 already reported, and shrinks the climate benefit too, since fewer marginal installations means less displaced oil-burning. Both numbers move the same direction: worse for the case that RETITC is well-targeted.

Separately, the minutes explicitly flag, in the "broader policy context" discussion, that "higher-income households tend to capture most solar credit benefits" — a claim owning a roof and having enough tax liability to use a nonrefundable credit structurally requires. And the Commission itself queued a **COST presentation on regressivity** for the next meeting (see below) — worth watching closely.

## Implications for Appleseed's published work

- **RETITC analysis**: Moore's own doubt about his 45-per-100 assumption is worth flagging in any update — it's a state-commissioned economist saying, on the record, that his interim number probably overstates the credit's effectiveness. Track the final UHERO report for a revised bottom-line figure below $0.71.
- **Budget Primer**: DBEDT's new 2026-Legislature mandate to evaluate tax credits and GET exemptions is new, citable context if the Primer discusses how the state currently reviews tax expenditures. The UHERO engagement is now a completed, voted commitment ($150,000 cap, unanimous roll call) rather than a pending proposal, if the Primer references the TRC's outside-research capacity.
- **Millionaire's tax report**: No direct connection in this packet. Chock's and White's outreach to Senate Ways & Means and House Finance is the channel through which TRC recommendations would reach the Legislature — worth watching once UHERO's tax-competitiveness work (which will eventually touch top-income-tax scenarios) produces findings.
